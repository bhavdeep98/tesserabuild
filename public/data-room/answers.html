The question

Close intelligence looks like enterprise SaaS; the Passport looks consumer. How do they connect — and why isn’t that three products?

Short answer

One product, one property dataset. Close intelligence is the capability that earns the right to capture and verify builder data to protect the close. That same verified stream is the birth of the Home Passport. Activation and care are capabilities on that identity — not separate products or a second data model.

Think of it as one pipeline under one identity, not three companies. The builder already holds what a homeowner will eventually need — what’s in the home, who installed it, what the warranty is, which permits were pulled. Today that information is scattered across construction, mortgage, title, CRM and finance systems, and most of it never reaches the homeowner as a continuous property record.

Close intelligence is why the builder pays us now to organize that data: to score on-time close risk across seams no single ERP owns. The Passport is not a later bet — it is what that verified stream becomes at handoff. Maintenance intelligence only works because the Passport already knows what was installed.

The question

Does the builder care that the passport exists? Is it more work for them — or are you solving their problem and building a valuable data set on the side?

Short answer

Nationals already ship homeowner apps (Pulte, Lennar, D.R. Horton). They do not need another binder UI. They need close risk visibility on the path to close — margin lost between construction, mortgage, and title — and a property-bound record that survives warranty and ownership change.

Close intelligence is the onboarding reason and economic proof (read-only, zero new field work). The Passport is the lasting asset produced by that same connection — and the channel every big builder still wants after closing.

The honest framing: we are not asking the builder to take on a data project for someone else’s future benefit. While we collect the data that becomes the Passport, we simultaneously give them the close-risk tool that justifies the spend on its own.

Owned UX apps already cover tickets and delight. What they do not create is a neutral identity that lenders, title, providers, and the next owner can trust after the builder’s login ends. That is the Passport gap Digs also leaves open when it forks a homeowner project at handoff.

Then comes post-close: every large builder still chases insurance, energy, and services relationships after the sale. Fragmented data is why those JVs stall. The Passport pulls the home into one verified string:

The question

Why do you win? Digs just raised $25.3M with Builders FirstSource. Why won’t a builder — or Digs — own this?

Short answer

Digs validates the category and the Carfax language — with real capital and distribution. Their architecture still forks a homeowner project at handoff; they do not span mortgage/title; the record is a document portal, not a parcel identity across owners.

A builder can ship UX (nationals already do). An ERP cannot cross the buyer-chosen lender boundary. We sit above that seam, read-only and vendor-neutral, with a team that has lived those gaps from inside a top builder.

Three structural reasons this does not collapse into “someone else ships a feature”:

It sits between systems — and it’s not an ERP

We don’t replace anyone’s system. We sit on top, read-only. The loss lives in the seams. A builder’s internal team lives inside one stack.

The lender boundary can’t be crossed internally

The buyer picks the lender, so the mortgage system changes deal to deal. No builder ERP reaches into lender and title on a neutral basis. A third party can.

Digs = portal + forked handoff

BFS-led Series A + five-year deal (Aug 25, 2026). Strong build-time AI. Handoff copies the binder into a new homeowner project. No close stack. That is the gap we own.

Nationals’ apps ≠ property identity

Pulte / Lennar / Horton can build homeowner experience. They cannot be the neutral record across lenders, title, competitors’ data, and the next owner.

Our real competitor used to look like a spreadsheet in the Monday pipeline meeting. Digs made the category loud. The race now is whether the industry defines the home record as a document portal or as a property identity through close and resale.

4
The question

Why blockchain / Ledger? Why isn’t a normal database enough?

Short answer

Because the value of the Passport is trust that outlives the builder, between parties who don’t inherently trust each other. The Ledger microservice stores a hash (fingerprint) of each record on a public chain — never documents, never PII — so anyone can verify a record was never tampered with after “warranty’s up, don’t call us.”

Close intelligence does not need a blockchain — we say that plainly. The chain earns its place beside Identity, where the record must be portable, multi-party, and permanent. A regular database owned by the builder works until the homeowner loses access to the builder’s system — which is exactly when they need the record.

How trust is built by default:

Off-chain data

Records stay in authorised stores. We never put personal data on-chain.

On-chain fingerprint

Only a hash per record — never documents, never PII. Proves the record hasn’t changed.

Verified by others

Self-reported until an independent party signs it. We provide the framework; we never become the sole verifier.

Analogy only: a Carfax-like permanence for the home. Digs and others already use that line for portals. Our claim is narrower — the Ledger keeps the Passport checkable after every login that created it is gone.
Scoping it honestly: we’re not defending blockchain on principle. Kestrel ships without it. It matters when the record must be trusted by parties who don’t trust each other and must outlive any single company’s systems — Identity + Ledger’s job, not the wedge’s.
