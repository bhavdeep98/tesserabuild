1
The question

Layer 1 looks like enterprise SaaS for builders; the passport looks consumer. How do the two connect — and why is one a wedge into the others?

Short answer

They connect because it is all one data set, captured once and reused three times. Layer 1 pulls the builder's fragmented data into one place to predict the close. That same verified data becomes the home's passport (Layer 2). And the passport is what makes maintenance intelligence possible (Layer 3). One is a wedge into the others because the later layers are literally built from the data the first layer creates — you can't start with the record or the maintenance, because neither exists until the builder data has been captured and verified.

Think of it as one pipeline, not three products. The builder has the same information a homeowner will eventually need — what's in the home, who installed it, what the warranty is, which permits were pulled. Today that information is scattered across BuildPro, Encompass, Qualia, Salesforce and JDE, and most of it never reaches the homeowner. Layer 1 is the reason to go get all of it, verify it, and organize it — because the builder pays us now to predict their closings from exactly that data.

So the sequence is not a roadmap of separate bets. It's a single flow where each stage hands its output to the next:

Layer 1 · The wedge

Predict

Pull fragmented builder data into one place. Score every home's on-time-close probability. The builder pays for this today.

→
Layer 2 · The record

Remember

That same verified data becomes the home's passport — a “Carfax for the home,” handed to the owner at closing.

→
Layer 3 · The service

Maintain

The passport lets us predict care — “your AC filter is due in 120 days,” “check your shingles in year two” — each citing its source.

This is also why we start with new construction, not existing homes. With a new build we capture the record at the source, accurate and verified. Retrofitting an existing home means plugging AI into someone's email to guess when the HVAC was last serviced — a data-entry mess. New construction is a clean data-entry problem we solve at the origin. (Existing homes via Zillow / Redfin data are a later expansion, not the wedge.)

2
The question

Does the builder care that the passport exists? Is it more work for them — or are you solving their problem and building a valuable data set on the side?

Short answer

The builder's built-in win is Layer 1 — real-time predictability and risk management on data they already own, with zero new work, because we're read-only and sit on top of their stack. The passport is a byproduct of doing that well, not extra work we ask of them.

And it hands them something every big builder is chasing and none has cracked: a clean way to monetize the homeowner after closing.

The honest framing is that we're not asking the builder to take on a data project for someone else's future benefit. While we collect the data for the passport, we're simultaneously giving them the predictability and risk tool that justifies the spend on its own. They don't have to build additional customer-care products on top of it — they take what they already have and get the chain for free.

Then comes the bigger prize: post-close monetization. Every large builder is trying to figure out how to upsell the homeowner after the sale — you see it in the joint ventures across the industry, in insurance and home-energy partnerships, in builders chasing another slice of the pie. Some have launched marketplaces; they tend to stall. The common reason is that the data needed to power one is fragmented, and no one agrees on how it ties together. The passport pulls it into one string.

No new customer-care stack

They reuse the data they already have. We layer predictability and the chain on top — nothing to build internally.

A working post-close channel

Maintenance requests route homeowner → trade directly, instead of homeowner → Lennar/Pulte/DHI as a warranty ticket.

Fast-tracked JVs

Having lived on the investment side of a builder, this cleans up the groundwork that makes insurance/services JVs easier to strike.

Optional crypto & syndication

Because records sit on a chain, it becomes an easier path to accept crypto or syndicate communities to investors — if the builder chooses.

Where we're honest about the open question: whether the builder takes a slice of the marketplace revenue, or whether that rev-share stays with the platform, is not yet decided — we're still doing the research on their existing JVs to model the benefit. It's a lever in the deal, not a settled number. Saying that plainly is stronger than pretending we've solved it.

3
The question

Why do you have the right to win? Some of the smartest people in the space could build their own — why won't they?

Short answer

It's less “right to win” and more why we will win: we've lived this. The team came from inside a top builder's operations and strategic-investment side. We've watched these exact gaps, we know what the builders' systems strategically can and cannot do, and we've figured out how to read across them. That cross-system view is the secret sauce — and it's not something you can hire overnight.

Two structural reasons a builder won't just build this internally:

It sits between systems — and it's not an ERP

We don't change or replace anyone's system. We sit on top, read-only. The value is in the seams no single system owns. A builder's internal team lives inside one stack; the loss lives between stacks.

The lender boundary can't be crossed internally

The buyer picks the lender, so the mortgage system changes deal to deal. No builder ERP can reach across into a lender's and a title company's systems on a neutral, read-only basis. A third party can.

And on the competitive landscape: the tools that exist today focus on construction lifecycle or the home's identity in isolation. Nobody combines cross-system closing prediction, a persistent verified record, and post-close intelligence for production builders. Our real competitor today is a spreadsheet — the pipeline meeting where four systems get merged by hand every Monday.

The blunt version: it's less a right to win than a reason we will. This team has lived these gaps from inside a top builder — we've watched them, we know what the incumbents' systems strategically can and cannot do to close them, and we've built the cross-system view that starts filling them. That's not something a competitor hires overnight.

4
The question

Why blockchain? Why isn't a normal database enough?

Short answer

Because the value of the record is trust that outlives the builder, between parties who don't inherently trust each other. We don't store anyone's data on-chain — we store a hash (a fingerprint) of each record on a public chain, so anyone can verify a record was never tampered with, and the record keeps proving itself long after Lennar or Pulte says “your warranty's up, don't call us.”

Layer 1 does not need a blockchain — we say that plainly. The chain earns its place at Layer 2, where the record has to be portable, multi-party, and permanent. A regular database owned by the builder works right up until the moment the homeowner no longer has access to the builder's system — which is exactly when they need the record (the insurance company calls after a tree falls; what shingles do I have?).

How the trust is built in by default:

Off-chain data

The actual records stay in the builder's own cloud. We never take custody of personal data.

On-chain fingerprint

Only a hash per record goes on a neutral public chain — never documents, never PII. It proves the record hasn't changed.

Verified by others

The record is self-reported until an independent party signs it. We provide the framework; we never become the verifier.

The plain-English test: it's a Carfax for the home. A record that's locked down, verifiable by anyone, and lives with the home — not the builder's login. That permanence and neutrality is the one thing a private database can't give you, and it's the reason each chain (title/ownership, construction lineage) carries its own standalone value.
Scoping it honestly: we're not defending blockchain on principle. Layer 1 ships without it. It matters when the record must be trusted by parties who don't trust each other and must outlive any single company's systems — and that's Layer 2's job, not the wedge's.
