The Logical Argument

Why the AI Predictability Engine is the wedge.

Tessera is a three-layer company — a Predictability Engine, a Home Passport, and Maintenance AI. This is not an argument that Layer 1 is the biggest layer. It is an argument that it is the only correct place to start — and that it is correct on every axis that matters.

Layer 1 is the strongest entry point — where the pain is most acute, adoption is lightest, the unit economics are structured to work, defensibility is earnable, and the data that lowers the cost of Layers 2 and 3 gets created. The wedge is Layer 1 because it’s the point where those advantages line up — a deliberate bet, with a named test behind each claim.

Priced so the decision is small — and structured to be tested cheaply.

Builders adopt software when the potential return is large relative to a small fee, the integration risk is low, and it fits how they already work. Tessera is built for all three — and we frame the return as a modeled case a paid pilot verifies, not a settled fact.

2
The Claim

The loss is predictable — and prediction is what converts it to savings.

A large loss only justifies a predictability engine if it can be seen coming with enough lead time to act. This is the load-bearing claim of the whole company.

The loss is not random — it is the output of a dependency structure. A home's path to close is a directed graph of milestones with fixed physical and legal ordering: construction phases run in one direction, lending has an irreducible minimum duration, a rate lock has an expiry, drywall gates the appraisal. Tessera encodes this as a 45-node, 48-edge graph — and 31% of those edges cross a system boundary no single vendor can see.

Because the structure is known…

Risk is visible before the outcome. “Construction done, lending not started” is detectable ~40 days out — arithmetic, not a surprise at closing.

Prediction is the mechanism.

A loss seen 3 days out is a loss. A loss seen 14+ days out is an intervention. The value lives entirely in the lead time.

Predict → Explain → Intervene.

Score every home, rank the dependency driving the risk in plain language, and surface the path to resolve before the close slips.

The honest boundary. Today the score is rule-based — a symbolic scaffold of graph constraints and cohort comparisons, returning provisional results, not a trained ML model. That is not a weakness hidden in the argument — it is the reason for the raise. The symbolic layer already produces defensible findings; the ML hire turns candidate patterns into calibrated probabilities. We name it before anyone finds it in the repo.
Conclusion A knowable dependency structure makes early warnings possible with useful lead time. A warning only becomes savings when someone can act in time — some risks (an unaffordable mortgage, an inspection backlog) are predictable yet unfixable. So prediction enables intervention; which interventions actually change outcomes, and by how much, is measured in a pilot — not assumed here.

The Distribution

Prediction is our preferred first layer to adopt.

Even a valuable product fails if it can't get in the door. Prediction is unusually adoptable, and it’s our chosen entry point — a strategic bet we’d test against a simpler handoff or warranty entry, not a logical necessity.

Read-only overlay — lower risk, not zero work

Tessera never writes back to builder systems; it sits above the stack, which answers “will this break my system of record?” It still requires access approval, field mapping, identity matching and a security review — real onboarding work, just without the risk of a rip-and-replace.

Fits a meeting they already hold

The status quo is a spreadsheet merged by hand every Monday for the pipeline review. Tessera fits that existing cadence rather than adding a new one — lighter adoption, though staff still have to trust and act on the alerts for value to appear.

5
The Moat — to be earned, not assumed

Defensibility comes from integration and permissioned history — not a legal exclusive.

A wedge anyone can copy is not a wedge. Our proposed advantage is spanning vendors under permitted access and compounding on the resulting history — a position we have to earn through coverage and speed, not a right no one else can obtain.

The deal is split across parties, and coordinating a read across all of them is genuinely hard. That difficulty is the opportunity — but it is not a legal exclusive. Authorization is required for us too, and a builder’s own captive mortgage and title units can reach across much of it:

Builder

Owns its construction / ERP stack — and often a captive lender and title unit (DHI’s mortgage arm handled ~82% of Q3 closings).

Lender

Varies deal to deal when the buyer chooses an outside lender, but is frequently the builder’s own affiliate.

Title & Escrow

Often a third party — one more permissioned integration to earn, not a barrier only we can pass.

Our read-only, non-competitive posture makes us easier to authorize across all three. The advantage we’re building is coverage and speed of integration plus the permissioned history that accrues — provable in deployments, not by decree:

Outcomes may compound into a data advantage

Each score can eventually be paired with the real outcome — on time, slipped, canceled — creating labeled examples, trained per-tenant so one builder’s data never trains another’s. The caveats are real: labels are incomplete, intervention changes the outcome, and separate tenant models don’t pool into a cross-builder edge. We’ll show learning curves and retention rather than assert a moat.

The relationship can lower the cost of Layers 2 & 3

Being inside the builder’s systems during construction can reduce the cost of assembling the Home Passport later. But reading a record is not the same as independently attesting it, and closing-status data is not the passport’s content (HVAC serials, manuals, warranties). What Layer 1 earns is a head start and permissioned provenance — which records carry forward, and with what rights, is something we prove field by field.

Conclusion Defensibility here is earnable, not automatic: coverage and speed of permissioned integration, plus the history that accrues, can compound into a sharper model and a lower-cost path to the larger layers. The moat is a hypothesis we intend to prove with deployments and retention — not a legal exclusive we claim today.

Pressure-Tested

The objections, answered.

A logical argument is only as strong as its treatment of the counter-arguments.

Why hasn't Lennar just built this internally?
They might — it’s a real risk, not something we rule out. The hard part is coordinating a read across builder, lender and title, and doing it as neutral infrastructure a buyer’s outside lender and title company will authorize. A builder with a captive lender can instrument much of its own flow; our bet is that a non-competitive, cross-vendor overlay is faster to deploy and easier to authorize than each builder rebuilding it — a claim we prove on deployment time and coverage.
What if Hyphen or ICE ships prediction as an ERP feature?
They already market intelligence across their products, so this is genuine competition, not a gap. Our proposed edge is cross-system lead time: a construction-only predictor doesn’t see the rate lock; a mortgage-only one doesn’t see the drywall. Whether the cross-boundary view meaningfully beats their native tools is a benchmark we owe, not an assumption we get to keep.
Isn't the score just rule-based today?
Yes — and we say so first. The symbolic scaffold already yields defensible constraint- and cohort-based findings. The ML hire turns those into calibrated probabilities. Naming the rule-based present as the reason for the hire is stronger than being caught claiming an ML capability the repo would contradict.
Why not blockchain first — isn't that the durable moat?
A permanent, portable, multi-party record is a Layer 2 concern. Layer 1 doesn't need it. Leading with the record before there's anything verified to record inverts the dependency and asks a conservative buyer to adopt the least urgent, least proven part first.
8
Intellectual Honesty

What would falsify this argument.

The argument is strong precisely because it names what would break it — and points to the experiments that decide.

If prediction lead time is short.

If, in real builder data, most slippage isn't visible until close, prediction can't convert loss to savings. The back-test targets — ≥14-day median lead time, ≥70% recall — exist to test exactly this.

If builders won't grant read access.

The model assumes read-only access to builder, lender, and title systems. Confirmed for Salesforce and Procore today; the rest is contingent. This is the single largest execution risk.

If a single-silo predictor is “good enough.”

If builders decide construction-only or mortgage-only prediction captures most of the value, the cross-system premium shrinks. The counter — that losses are dominated by cross-boundary dependencies — is a claim to prove on real outcomes, not assume.

If warnings can’t be acted on in time.

A correct alert only creates value if an owner has a feasible action and enough runway. If most flagged risks are predictable-but-unfixable (unaffordable mortgage, inspection backlog), the recoverable share collapses. The pilot logs actions and net cost — not just alert accuracy.

If loaded gross margin is thin.

The 98% figure is infrastructure-only. Once onboarding, connector maintenance, data services and support labor are loaded in, margin could be materially lower. We report gross margin only after those costs are counted.
