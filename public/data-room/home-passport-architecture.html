01 · The concept

What Tessera HomePassport is.

Three parties touch a home and none of them fully trust each other: the builder who built it, the owner who lives in it, and the providers who service it. HomePassport sits between them and does three jobs.

One home identity

A stable, opaque ID for the home that outlives every owner, builder and vendor. The plaque points to it — it does not unlock it.

A continuous history

Equipment, work, warranties and documents — each fact naming its source and whether it is reported, verified, disputed or corrected. It travels with the home to the next owner.

Authorized services

The owner turns on internet, utilities and trades in a tap. Providers get only what one job needs, for a limited time, and return an attributed result.

The reason all three can accept a middle party is that the data is walled into three separate stores — the wall is the product.

Builder workspace

Stays with the builder

Cost, margin, pipeline. Only an approved home projection is published outward.

Home history

Follows the home

Equipment, work, eligible warranties. Relevant history transfers at resale.

Personal vault

Follows the person

Contact, payment, service accounts. Never auto-transfers to the next owner.

02 · The architecture

One protected door, three stores, a ledger underneath.

Everyone enters through one protected gate. Inside a dedicated environment, an application layer checks authority, limits data to exactly what is allowed, and coordinates approved work. The database and document storage are encrypted with per-person keys; a separate security account holds audit and backups; the ledger underneath receives only tamper-evidence. 03 · How activation happens

In one line.

Activating a home = the verified owner approves sharing a few home facts with a provider and approves the order, and Tessera dispatches one tracked job — no address to type, nothing sensitive copied out.

It stays one tap because the identity proof happened earlier, at closing — so the tap only confirms an already-verified owner acting on an already-known home. The full step-by-step (claim → share → approve → dispatch), with every edge case, lives in the use-case catalog.

05 · Our threat model

How we stress-tested the design.

Lawyers are welcome to review; our contribution is threat modelling. For each way the system could betray trust, we name the attack and the one rule that makes it structurally hard — not merely discouraged. This is the data-security position.

Attack · impersonate an owner

Claim a home you don’t own

Scan a plaque, guess an ID, or reuse an invite to seize a home’s record.

✓Locator ≠ credential. Ownership needs verified closing evidence + identity; unbound lookups return a plain not-found; address→home resolution sits behind auth.
Attack · over-collect at a provider

Pull more than the job needs

A provider (or a hostile provider account) tries to read fields or homes outside its job.

✓Lend, don’t send. Payload is built from the grant; sensitive fields shown in a watermarked time-boxed view; only the minimum (usually the address) transfers.
Attack · defeat deletion

Make erasure impossible

Append-only history and an immutable ledger seem to block a right-to-erasure request forever.

✓Crypto-shred. Personal data is encrypted per person; erasure destroys the key across live storage and backups. Property facts are de-linked and stay.
Attack · read a person off the ledger

Recover PII from the chain

Someone holding the ledger tries to work backwards to a name or address.

✓Hashes only, salted. Personal fields are pointers; the secret salt lives with the owner’s export. After erasure the commitment is anonymous by construction.
Attack · duplicate or silent purchase

Order twice, or share becomes buy

A timeout retries into a second order; a data share is treated as a purchase.

✓Two approvals + one durable job. Sharing and buying are separate consents; a stable job key blocks duplicates; ambiguous outcomes go to review.
Attack · stale power after sale

Old owner keeps access

A former owner acts on a home after closing, or an old grant still works.

✓Versioned authority. Transfer atomically bumps the ownership epoch and kills every prior grant. Every request is checked against current authority.
Attack · rogue insider

An admin bypasses the walls

A privileged operator reads content or destroys data at scale.

✓No standing access. Support is time-boxed and dual-approved; the audit archive is write-once with separate admins; bulk key destruction needs dual control. Residual risk is documented, not hidden.
Attack · dense-PII documents

Leak an ID or closing packet

An identity document lingers and turns a breach into identity theft.

✓Tier + discard. Documents are classified at intake; identity evidence is attested then discarded or sealed under mandatory retention; nothing reaches a durable store unlabelled.

Four choices must be right before the first record is anchored, because they can’t be retrofitted: personal fields as pointers, two salt classes, per-person encryption, and classification at intake. get these right on day one

One home, one record: claimed by proven ownership, activated in one honest tap, where sensitive data is lent not sent, personal data can be truly deleted, and the ledger holds a fingerprint, never you.
